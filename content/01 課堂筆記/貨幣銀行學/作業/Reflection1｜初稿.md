# Presentation Reflection #1

Eric Tsu (祖曜筬) · B14703001 · Fin 2007 Money and Banking (a)

## 1. Group 20 — The Run on Silicon Valley Bank

The main lesson I took from this presentation is that SVB failed when two ordinary risks hit at the same time: interest-rate risk on its assets and run risk on its deposits. Between 2019 and 2021 its deposits rose from $61.8 billion to $189.2 billion. Loan demand was weak, so the bank put the money into long-term securities: 55% of its assets were securities, against 25% for peers, and its held-to-maturity bonds had an average duration of 6.2 years. When the Fed raised rates, those bonds lost value, but held-to-maturity accounting kept the losses out of reported equity. The group's distinction between solvency and liquidity made this clear to me: the paper losses only became fatal once depositors wanted their cash back.

The deposit side surprised me more. 94% of SVB's deposits were uninsured, and most came from startups linked through the same venture-capital network. These depositors received the same news through the same channels, so about $42 billion left in a single day. This connects to what we studied about the safety net: deposit insurance prevents runs only when most deposits are covered.

In my view, the supervisory failure deserves as much attention as the bank's own mistakes. The Fed's review found that supervisors saw the weaknesses but did not escalate in time, and the chief risk officer position was vacant for most of 2022. Rules on paper matter only if supervisors act on what they find.

One suggestion for the group: a short calculation would have linked the story to our duration analysis. A portfolio with a duration of 6.2 loses roughly 12% of its value when rates rise by two percentage points.

## 2. Group 18 — 1997 and LTCM: When a Convergence Trade Goes Wrong

The key lesson from this presentation is that a trade can be right about value and still fail, because the funding can run out before prices converge. At the start of 1998, LTCM held about $120 billion of positions on $4.8 billion of equity, a leverage of 25 times. At that leverage, a 4% loss on the positions wipes out all of the equity.

What I learned most from the group was how the losses spread. 82% of LTCM's losses came from relative-value trades that looked different but shared one exposure: they were long less-liquid claims and short safe, liquid ones. After Russia's default in August 1998, investors fled to quality and these spreads widened together, so diversification failed. Equity fell to $2.3 billion by August 31 and $0.6 billion by September 23, which pushed leverage above 50 times and triggered margin calls. The group's feedback loop slide showed how market risk and funding risk reinforce each other: wider spreads cause losses, losses cause margin calls, and forced sales widen spreads further.

I found the contrast with the 1997 Nobel Prize the most interesting part. Merton and Scholes' models answer what a claim is worth, while LTCM failed on a different question: can the portfolio stay funded? The same applies to any investor who trades on margin, since a margin call forces selling at the worst price.

One suggestion: the group could have discussed the rescue more. The New York Fed used no public money, but it organized a $3.625 billion private recapitalization by 14 firms. This raises the moral-hazard question we covered in class: if the central bank steps in to arrange a rescue, do large leveraged funds learn to expect one next time?

## 3. Group 14 — Are Taiwan's Interest Rates Too Low?

What I valued most in this presentation is that the group defined "too low" before answering. A rate is too low under their definition when the real rate is negative and the economy is running well above normal. They also measured the real rate using the overnight interbank rate (about 0.82%) and core CPI, instead of the 2.00% rediscount rate, since the interbank rate is what banks actually pay. With this definition, rates were not too low from 2024Q2 to 2025Q3, but they became too low from 2025Q4, when GDP growth reached 12.9–15.4%, bank lending grew 10.3%, and the real overnight rate fell to about −1.4%. Their estimated gap of 50–120 basis points gave a concrete answer.

I agree that policy is loose, but I think the central bank's position is stronger than the group suggested. The growth is concentrated in AI-related sectors, while traditional sectors remain weak, so a broad rate hike would fall hardest on the sectors that are not overheating. A second constraint comes from the group's own data: life insurers hold about NT$9.26 trillion of unhedged foreign assets. If higher rates strengthened the NT dollar, those insurers would face large currency losses. The same exposure that low rates created now makes raising rates risky, which helps explain why the central bank prefers targeted credit controls.

The comparison with South Korea was useful, since both economies are export-oriented, semiconductor-driven and aging. One suggestion: the slide comparing their timing listed Taiwan at "2.3% growth and 11% inflation", which looks like the two numbers were swapped. Checking figures like this would make a strong argument even more convincing.
